# Reading taste profile

This is a working model, not a set of permanent rules. Strong reactions to individual books are recorded in `books/`; this file keeps only the patterns that matter across recommendations.

## Strong preferences

- Page-turning stories with early momentum
- Experiential immersion: stories that make the reader feel as if they are living events rather than watching them from outside
- Worlds that feel coherent, inhabited, and understandable, including enough everyday texture that they seem to exist beyond the plot
- Plausible technology, preferably near- or medium-future
- Concrete constraints and problems that let the reader mentally participate in the characters' reasoning and solutions
- Competent characters solving concrete problems
- Humor rooted in character rather than references or memes
- Clear prose that can be genuinely strong or elevated without becoming showy or laborious
- Big ideas presented through story rather than lectures
- Speculative systems with clear, internally coherent rules
- Characters who evolve believably under pressure and across long arcs
- Antagonists with understandable motives and psychological depth
- Strange premises that earn emotional investment through disciplined execution
- Speculative ideas that create meaningful consequences for character, society, or both

## Common failure modes

- Long openings before the central story begins
- Narrative distance that makes events feel observed rather than experienced
- Worlds that function as plot machinery without enough lived-in social, practical, or sensory texture
- Invented vocabulary introduced without enough context
- Far-future technology that feels indistinguishable from magic
- Speculative mechanisms that become magical or wish-fulfilling without a concrete basis
- Shallow nerd-reference humor
- Manchild protagonists
- Thin, clichéd, or unrelatable characters
- Characters who feel like functional roles in the premise rather than people inhabiting the world
- Elaborate concepts with weak character development
- Stories built around a premise that is difficult to believe
- Dense prose that makes recreational reading feel like work
- Aggressively fragmented prose or one-sentence-per-line formatting that calls attention to itself
- Detailed descriptions of abstract games or systems that are difficult to visualize
- Predictable plots when the central premise is already straining suspension of disbelief
- Spectacle or horror used decoratively rather than in service of the story
- Familiar speculative tropes repeated without enough new conceptual or character value
- Bold social extrapolation that feels arbitrary, dated, or provocative without being convincingly earned

## Important nuances

- Literary prestige is not necessary. A great story can outweigh ordinary prose, as demonstrated by `The Expanse`.
- Stronger prose is a positive, not merely something to tolerate. `The First Fifteen Lives of Harry August` showed that noticeably elevated writing can improve the experience when it remains transparent and easy to read.
- Fast, cinematic storytelling is not automatically shallow. `Recursion` worked as a Hollywood blockbuster because its world remained coherent and its characters changed believably.
- The same cinematic style can become a weakness when the concept feels recycled or the formatting becomes intrusive, as in `Dark Matter` after `Recursion`.
- Character depth matters more than earlier recommendations assumed. `Replay` and `Harry August` both benefited from believable long-term character arcs, while thin characterization weakened `Jurassic Park` and `Sphere`.
- Experiential immersion may be one of the strongest predictors of a hit. `The Martian` works because the loneliness, constraints, failures, and intelligent solutions are experienced alongside Watney; the reader is invited to solve the problems with him rather than merely watch him solve them.
- This same "living the story" quality helps explain the success of `Project Hail Mary`, `The Gone World`, `The Expanse`, and `Harry August`, despite very different settings and styles.
- A believable world is not just a logically consistent premise. The reader should be able to imagine ordinary life inside it. Social habits, jobs, physical constraints, institutions, relationships, and sensory details help turn a setting from a diagram into a place.
- Narrative orientation is part of immersion. Unexplained terminology or missing context can abruptly push the reader out of the scene and into decoding mode.
- Horror is not a general preference, but it can work extremely well when placed deliberately and supported by the story, as in `The Gone World`.
- Complexity is welcome when the reader remains oriented. Convoluted rules are acceptable; unexplained nouns and vocabulary cliffs are not.
- Suspension of disbelief is a major decision point. Outrageous premises can work when the rules feel concrete and earned; apparently more conventional premises can fail when the worldbuilding or mechanism feels hand-waved.
- A bizarre or initially cringey premise can work when the execution is rigorous, emotionally credible, and internally consistent.
- Humor must emerge from character and circumstance. References and memes are not substitutes for wit.
- A fascinating setting does not automatically rescue an unconvincing central story engine.
- Repeated-life and time-manipulation stories are a strong fit when they introduce genuinely different mechanics or consequences rather than repeating the same wish-fulfillment beats.
- A page-turner can still produce deep ambivalence. `The Forever War` was compelling and memorable despite social extrapolations and an ending that seriously strained acceptance; numerical ratings should not be forced when they flatten the reaction.
- Michael Crichton's style may simply be a middling fit. Both `Jurassic Park` and `Sphere` landed at 7/10 for related reasons: suspension-of-disbelief problems, weaker characterization, and speculative mechanisms or worldbuilding that did not fully convince. A likely deeper reason is that his stories can feel observed from outside: the premise and system are visible, but the reader does not always feel fully embedded in the characters' lived experience.
- Familiarity with a film adaptation can reduce discovery and surprise, but `Sphere` suggests that prior familiarity was not the only reason `Jurassic Park` underperformed expectations.
