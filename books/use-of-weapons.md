# Use of Weapons

## Current impression

A bit more than halfway through: a good book, but not a great one. The prose and seriousness are not the problem. The main disappointment is that the Culture universe is unimaginably large and technologically godlike, yet much of the lived world remains surprisingly human-centered, familiar, and underexplained.

## What worked

- Serious, controlled writing
- The Culture remains intrinsically interesting
- Vast scale and near-godlike technology create strong potential for discovery
- The book is clearly substantial work, far above lightweight or shallow science fiction

## What is not working

- Enormous galactic scale does not produce enough genuinely alien environments, ecologies, atmospheres, gravities, organisms, or phenomena
- Many planets feel like variations of Earth despite existing inside a pan-galactic civilization
- The Culture itself remains tantalizingly underexplained: its origins, motivations, history, and connective tissue are mostly delivered in tiny slices
- Local societies can seem to exist in isolation from the larger technological reality around them
- The book often presents a new society for a few chapters, then moves on before that world feels fully connected to the larger universe
- Characters feel relatively bland and static rather than accumulating pressure, moral change, or personal transformation
- New settings provide scenery more often than they challenge or reshape the characters

## Reader comments

> It's a good book, but it's not a great book.

> The author creates this unfathomably large universe, it's pan galactic, distances basically don't apply anymore, technology is omnipotent to the point of being close to godlike.

> Fetile soil for absolutely bonker discoveries, right?

> But no. It's completely human centric. Planets are always loke earth. No talk about different gravities or atmospheres. No weird critters, weird plants, unexpected phenomena. Each planet is like earth, except you got magical technology with you.

> Basically, each scene feels like 'Drop a human into a new society for 3 chapters, then go to the next one'.

> It's still a serious piece of work. There's just too much left to explain in the universe and you're left holding the bag.

## Recommendation implications

### Recommend more of

- Worldbuilding where scale produces genuinely different environments, species, constraints, and cultures
- Settings where technology, biology, geography, and history causally shape everyday life
- Universes with enough connective tissue to explain how local societies fit into the larger technological and political reality
- Characters who accumulate consequences and are changed by the places, dangers, and moral pressures they encounter

### Avoid

- Galactic scale used mainly as scenery
- Human societies lightly reskinned as alien worlds
- Fascinating macro-settings that leave too much of their basic functioning implicit
- Episodic travel through societies without enough causal connection between them
- Static protagonists who function mainly as cameras for new settings

## Context

This reaction is provisional because the book is only a little more than halfway finished. It also reinforces concerns from `The Player of Games`: Banks' Culture is fascinating in abstraction, but the method of worldbuilding may be less immersive than preferred.
